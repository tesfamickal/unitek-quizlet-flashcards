1. Big-picture mental model

The chapter connects two systems that work together:

System Main idea What to picture
Lymphatic system Returns excess tissue fluid to the bloodstream, filters lymph, absorbs dietary fats, and houses immune cells. A one-way drainage/filtering network without its own pump.
Immune system Recognizes foreign or dangerous material and protects the body. A layered defense system: barriers first, then cells/chemicals, then targeted antigen-antibody responses.
Oncology connection Cancer can use lymph vessels/nodes to spread, and cancer treatments often involve immune/lymphatic effects. Tumor → invasion → lymph nodes → metastasis.

High-yield phrase:

Blood circulates in a pumped loop; lymph moves in a one-way return path back toward blood, helped by body movement and muscle contraction.

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  2. Identify specialists who treat lymphatic, immune, infectious, and cancer disorders.
  3. Recognize, define, spell, and pronounce the primary terms for lymphatic/immune structures, functions, pathology, diagnostic tests, and treatments.
  4. Recognize, define, spell, and pronounce primary oncology terms.

Practical study translation:

  • Draw the lymph route from tissue fluid back to the bloodstream.
  • Explain how B cells, T cells, NK cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, cytokines, interferons, interleukins, antibodies, and complement differ.
  • Match each specialist to the problem they treat.
  • Sort infections by organism type.
  • Explain tumor spread, staging, and treatment options.

3. Functions of the lymphatic system

The book gives three core lymphatic-system functions.

Function What it means Key terms
Absorb fats and fat-soluble vitamins Lacteals in the villi of the small intestine absorb fats that cannot travel directly through the bloodstream at first. lacteals, villi, fat-soluble vitamins
Remove waste and cooperate with immunity Lymph picks up waste, protein molecules, pathogens, cell debris, and abnormal cells from tissue spaces. interstitial fluid, lymph, pathogens
Return filtered lymph to venous blood Lymph travels through vessels and nodes, then returns to veins at the base of the neck. lymphatic vessels, lymph nodes, ducts

Fat absorption: villi and lacteals

  • Villi are tiny fingerlike projections lining the small intestine.
  • Each villus contains blood vessels and a lacteal.
  • Lacteals are specialized lymphatic structures that absorb dietary fats and fat-soluble vitamins.
  • These fats are transformed in lacteal cells and eventually returned to venous circulation for body-wide use.

Memory hook:

Lacteals load lipids into lymph.

4. Interstitial fluid → lymph creation

Interstitial fluid

Interstitial fluid, also called intercellular tissue fluid, is plasma-derived fluid that leaves arterial blood capillaries and moves into spaces between cells.

Jobs of interstitial fluid:

  • Delivers oxygen, nutrients, and hormones to cells.
  • Picks up waste products, proteins, debris, and substances created by cells.
  • Most returns directly to the bloodstream; the remaining portion enters lymphatic capillaries.

Lymph

Lymph is the remaining interstitial fluid once it enters lymphatic capillaries.

Lymph contains:

  • electrolytes
  • proteins
  • waste products
  • pathogens or foreign material
  • cancer cells in some disease states
  • lymphocytes and other immune-related cells

Flow map:

blood plasma leaves capillaries
→ interstitial fluid between cells
→ lymphatic capillaries
→ lymph
→ larger lymphatic vessels
→ lymph nodes filter it
→ lymphatic ducts
→ venous blood near base of neck

Key distinction:

Before entering lymph vessels After entering lymph vessels
interstitial fluid lymph

5. Lymphatic circulation vs blood circulation

Feature Blood circulatory system Lymphatic system
Pump Heart pumps blood. No pump-like organ.
Direction Looped circulation. One-way upward return to blood.
Fluid visibility Blood is red and vessels are more visible. Lymph is clear, vessels are harder to see.
Filtration Blood waste is filtered by kidneys. Lymph is filtered by lymph nodes.
Movement Pressure from heart. Skeletal-muscle movement/body motion and vessel valves.

Important rule:

Once lymph enters a lymphatic capillary, it must keep moving forward/upward toward return to the bloodstream.

6. Lymphatic capillaries and vessels

Lymphatic capillaries

  • Microscopic, blind-ended tubes near the surface of the body.
  • Capillary walls are only one cell thick.
  • Cells briefly separate to allow lymph to enter.
  • When the cells close again, lymph is pushed forward.
  • They merge into progressively larger lymphatic vessels.

Figure idea from the book: lymph capillaries sit near blood capillaries and tissue cells. Fluid leaves blood capillaries, bathes tissues, then enters lymph capillaries.

Lymphatic vessels

  • Carry lymph from capillaries toward lymph nodes and ducts.
  • Depend on surrounding muscle motion because lymphatics do not have a central pump.
  • Contain valves that support one-way flow.

Word parts:

Word part Meaning
lymph/o lymph
lymphangi/o lymphatic vessel
angi/o vessel

7. Lymphocytes and immune cells

The chapter describes three major lymphocyte groups:

  1. Natural killer cells
  2. B cells
  3. T cells

Antigen

An antigen is any substance the body regards as foreign. Examples include:

  • viruses
  • bacteria
  • toxins
  • transplanted tissues
  • abnormal/cancer cells

Lymphocyte maturation and differentiation

  • Maturation = becoming mature.
  • Differentiation = becoming modified/specialized for a particular function.
  • Lymphocytes mature and differentiate in lymphoid tissues.

Natural killer cells

Natural killer cells help kill:

  • cancer cells
  • cells infected by viruses

They are part of fast immune defense and do not require the same exact antigen-specific setup as B/T cells.

B cells

  • Also called B lymphocytes.
  • Produce antibodies.
  • Each B cell makes antibodies coded for a specific antigen.
  • When activated, B cells can transform into plasma cells.
  • Plasma cells secrete large volumes of antibodies.

T cells

  • Also called T lymphocytes.
  • The “T” connects to the thymus, where they mature.
  • T cells are central to cell-mediated immunity.
  • They release signaling proteins and help direct other immune cells.

Cytokines, interferons, and interleukins

Term Main job
Cytokines Protein signals released mainly by T cells; act like immune-system messages.
Interferons Produced in response to antigens, especially viruses and tumor cells; activate immune defenses, slow viral multiplication, and signal other cells.
Interleukins Help direct B and T cells to divide and proliferate.

8. Additional lymphatic structures

The remaining lymphatic structures are made of lymphoid tissue, meaning tissue resembling lymph or lymphatic tissue. Their main roles work with immunity.

Figure 6.3 labels included:

  • tonsil/adenoid
  • lymphatic vessels
  • thymus
  • spleen
  • lymph nodes
  • bone marrow
  • skin
  • vermiform appendix

Tonsils

The tonsils form a protective ring near the entrance to the respiratory and digestive tracts.

Tonsil group Location/function
Adenoids / nasopharyngeal tonsils Located in the nasopharynx, upper part of the pharynx.
Palatine tonsils Left and right sides of the throat; visible at the back of the mouth. palatine refers to hard/soft palate.
Lingual tonsils Base of tongue; not readily visible. lingual = tongue-related.

Main function:

Tonsils help prevent pathogens from entering through the nose and mouth.

Thymus gland

  • Mass of lymphoid tissue located above the heart.
  • Reaches greatest size around puberty.
  • Becomes smaller with age.
  • Secretes a hormone that stimulates lymphocytes to mature into T cells.
  • T cells leave the thymus through blood and lymphatic systems.

Spleen

  • Located in the left upper abdomen.
  • Connected to both immune and cardiovascular functions.
  • Filters microorganisms and foreign material from blood.
  • A ruptured spleen is a medical emergency.
  • Splenomegaly = abnormal enlargement of the spleen.
  • Splenorrhagia = bleeding from the spleen.

Bone marrow

  • Produces blood cells, including leukocytes/immune cells.
  • Lymphocytes are formed in bone marrow before later maturation/specialization.

Appendix

  • Lymphoid structure associated with the cecum.
  • May participate in immune activity.

9. First line of defense

The immune system is not one single organ. It uses structures from multiple body systems.

First-line defenses include:

Defense How it helps
Intact skin Physical barrier; acid mantle makes surface less hospitable to bacteria.
Respiratory system Nose hairs and mucus trap foreign material; coughing/sneezing expel irritants/pathogens.
Digestive system Stomach acids and enzymes destroy swallowed invaders.
Lymphatic system and leukocytes Attack and destroy pathogens that pass surface barriers.

Important wording:

  • Intact skin means skin without cuts, scrapes, sores, or breaks.
  • If pathogens get through the first line, the immune system moves to more specific responses.

10. Antigen-antibody reaction

An antigen-antibody reaction, also called an immune reaction, is the binding of antigens to antibodies.

What this does:

  1. Labels potentially dangerous antigens.
  2. Helps immune cells recognize them.
  3. Helps neutralize or flag them for destruction.
  4. Allows macrophages and other cells to remove them.

Antibody

An antibody is a disease-fighting protein created by the immune system in response to a specific antigen.

Other name: immunoglobulin.

Word-part clue:

  • anti- = against
  • antibody = “body/protein against” an antigen

Tolerance

Tolerance means acquired unresponsiveness to a specific antigen. It can also describe decreased drug response after repeated use.

11. Monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, and complement

Immune component Role
Monocytes Leukocytes that provide immune defenses against many organisms; become macrophages/dendritic cells after leaving blood and entering tissues.
Macrophages Large phagocytic leukocytes that surround and eat invading cells; remove dead cells; stimulate other immune cells.
Dendritic cells Patrol for antigens; when found, they swallow them and alert B and T cells.
Complement system Blood proteins that help antibodies destroy pathogens by dissolving/removing bacteria and foreign cells.

Word parts:

Word part Meaning
macro- large
-phage / phag/o eat, swallow
complement complete / make whole

12. Immunity types

Immunity means resistance to a specific disease.

Type How protection is obtained Example
Natural/passive immunity Protection exists without deliberate antigen administration; can be augmented by maternal antibodies. Breast milk passes protection from nursing mother to baby.
Acquired immunity Protection obtained after exposure or vaccination. Having had a contagious disease; vaccination for measles/polio.
Vaccination Uses whole/partial killed/weakened disease-causing organisms or antigens to stimulate protection. MMR vaccine.
Booster Periodic repeat dose needed for some vaccines to maintain protection. Booster schedules.

Course caution: your Canvas quiz may use exact wording such as active vs acquired. Conceptually, vaccines train the body’s own immune response; for Canvas, match the course wording when available.

14. Lymphatic-system pathology and diagnostics

Lymphangioma

  • Benign tumor/malformation of lymphatic vessels.
  • Often congenital.
  • Word parts: lymphangi/o + -oma.

Ruptured spleen

  • Tearing of spleen covering, usually from abdominal trauma.
  • Medical emergency.

Splenomegaly

  • Abnormal enlargement of the spleen.
  • May be associated with injury/bleeding, infectious disease such as mononucleosis, or abnormal immune function.

Lymphoscintigraphy

  • Diagnostic test for damaged or malformed lymphatic vessels.
  • A radioactive substance is injected into lymph ducts.
  • Scanner/probe follows movement on a computer screen.

15. Lymphedema

Lymphedema is swelling caused by abnormal lymph accumulation in tissues because lymph drainage is not working properly.

Important contrast:

Lymphedema Regular injury swelling
Lymph/fluid builds up because lymph drainage fails. Swelling from injury such as sprain is not necessarily lymphatic failure.

Why it matters:

  • Lymph is protein-rich.
  • Protein-rich fluid can support pathogen growth.
  • Lymphedema is often associated with infections.

Types

Type Cause/pattern
Primary lymphedema Hereditary condition; often begins in feet and progresses upward; more common in females; symptoms may appear around puberty.
Secondary lymphedema Caused by damage to lymphatic vessels, often from cancer treatment, surgery, trauma, burns, or injury.

Treatment/management

  • Compression garments such as socks, stockings, sleeves, and bandages.
  • Exercise to support pressure and movement of lymph.
  • Goal is control/reduction, not cure.
  • Bioimpedance spectroscopy can help diagnose early fluid buildup by measuring resistance to electrical current through the affected limb.

16. Allergies and allergic response

Allergy basics

  • Allergy = immune overreaction to an allergen.
  • Allergen = substance that produces allergic reaction.
  • Examples include pollen, foods, medications, insect stings, and environmental triggers.

Anaphylaxis

Also called:

  • systemic reaction
  • anaphylactic shock

Anaphylaxis is a severe response to an allergen that can rapidly involve several body systems. Without prompt treatment, the patient can die quickly.

Possible signs shown in the figure/context:

  • bronchial constriction / breathing difficulty
  • hives
  • hypotension
  • systemic symptoms after trigger such as bee sting

Scratch test

  • Allergens are placed on skin.
  • Skin is scratched and each allergen is labeled.
  • Reactions often begin within about 20 minutes.
  • Used to identify troublesome allergens such as pollen or ragweed.

IgE test

  • Allergen-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) blood test can help identify allergy to a particular substance.

Antihistamines

  • Relieve or prevent allergy symptoms.
  • Work by blocking/preventing effects of histamine.
  • Histamine contributes to itching, sneezing, runny nose, watery eyes, and other allergic symptoms.

17. Autoimmune disorders

An autoimmune disorder/disease happens when the immune system produces antibodies against the body’s own tissues, mistaking healthy cells/tissues/organs for antigens.

General patterns from the book:

  • Some autoimmune tendency appears genetically transmitted.
  • More common in women during childbearing years.
  • Can affect many body systems.

Examples of autoimmune disorders by body system

Body system Example What it affects
Skeletal Rheumatoid arthritis Joints and connective tissue.
Muscular Myasthenia gravis Nerve and muscle synapses.
Cardiovascular Pernicious anemia Red blood cells.
Digestive Crohn’s disease Intestines, ileum, or colon.
Nervous Multiple sclerosis Brain and spinal cord.
Integumentary Scleroderma Skin and connective tissues.
Endocrine Graves’ disease Thyroid gland.

Memory hook:

Autoimmune = immune system attacks “self.”

18. Immunodeficiency disorders, HIV, and AIDS

Immunodeficiency

An immunodeficiency disorder occurs when immune response is compromised. Compromised means weakened or not functioning properly.

SCID

Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID):

  • Inherited condition.
  • Immune abnormalities cause high infection susceptibility.
  • Can be fatal from infections.
  • Now often included in infant newborn screening.

HIV

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV):

  • Blood-borne infection.
  • Damages or kills T cells.
  • Weakens immune system over time.
  • Can be transmitted perinatally, sexually, or behaviorally through unprotected sex/sharing needles.
  • Current prevention and treatment make transmission much more preventable than in the past.

AIDS

Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS):

  • Most advanced/fatal stage of HIV infection.
  • Associated with opportunistic infections and cancers.

Opportunistic infection

An opportunistic infection is caused by a pathogen that does not usually cause illness in healthy people but can cause disease when the host is debilitated or immunocompromised.

Kaposi’s sarcoma

  • Cancer frequently associated with HIV/AIDS.
  • Causes abnormal tissue patches under skin, in mouth, nose, throat, or other organs.

HIV tests

Test Use
ELISA Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay; screens for HIV antibodies and other infectious conditions such as Lyme disease.
HIV differentiation assay Used to confirm a positive ELISA test.

19. Antibody therapy and immunotherapy

Immunotherapy / biological therapy

Immunotherapy is treatment that stimulates or represses the immune response.

Two uses:

Situation Goal
Cancer Stimulate immune response to fight malignancy.
Allergy Repress sensitivity to an allergen; also called allergy desensitization or allergen immunotherapy.

Antibody therapy

Therapy Main idea
Synthetic immunoglobulins / immune serum Post-exposure preventive measure for some viruses, including rabies and hepatitis; provides temporary immunity after exposure.
Synthetic interferon Used in treatment of multiple sclerosis, hepatitis C, and some cancers.
Monoclonal antibodies Lab-produced antibodies from a clone of specific cells; can enhance immune response against some malignancies including non-Hodgkin lymphoma, melanoma, breast cancer, and colon cancer.

Word clue:

  • Monoclonal = one clone.

20. Pathogens: bacteria, fungi, viruses, parasites

A pathogen is a disease-producing organism. The chapter emphasizes sorting diseases by organism type.

Type Main idea Examples from chapter
Bacteria Single-celled organisms; some harmless, some pathogenic. anthrax, rickettsia, shigella, Lyme disease spirochete, staphylococci, streptococci, MRSA
Fungi/yeast Simple parasitic organisms; some harmless, some pathogenic. tinea pedis/athlete’s foot, aspergillosis, candidiasis, oral thrush, vaginal candidiasis
Viruses Infect cells; many must be prevented/controlled rather than treated with antibiotics. Ebola, influenza, measles, mumps, rubella, rabies, West Nile, Zika, herpesviruses
Parasites Live within/on another organism. malaria, toxoplasmosis

21. Bacterial infections and antibiotic resistance

Bacterial examples

Term Study point
Bacillus anthracis Spores grown in laboratories have been used in biological warfare.
Rickettsia Small bacteria living in lice, fleas, ticks, mites; Rocky Mountain spotted fever is transmitted by infected tick bite.
Spirochetes Long, slender, spiral-shaped bacteria with flexible walls and movement.
Shigellosis Caused by Shigella; causes diarrhea and fever; prevented with handwashing and hygiene.
Lyme disease Tick-borne infection from Borrelia burgdorferi; named for Old Lyme, Connecticut; symptoms can include fever, headache, fatigue, bullseye rash; can spread to joints, heart, nervous system if untreated.
Staphylococci Cluster-forming spherical bacteria; many harmless, some severe.
Staphylococcus aureus Can infect wounds and cause food poisoning.
Streptococci Chain-forming spherical bacteria; can cause strep throat, meningitis, endocarditis, necrotizing fasciitis.

Antibiotic-resistant bacteria

Antibiotic-resistant bacteria occur when antibiotics fail to kill all target bacteria. Surviving bacteria become resistant, infections last longer, and complications/death risk increases.

MRSA

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA):

  • Resistant to many antibiotics.
  • Early skin sign can look like small red bumps with black top.
  • Can become abscesses needing immediate care.
  • Serious, hard to treat, sometimes fatal.
  • Often enters through skin breaks.
  • Increasingly present in the general population, not only hospitals.

22. Fungus and yeast infections

Fungus

A fungus is a simple parasitic organism; plural = fungi.

Examples:

Condition Meaning
Tinea pedis / athlete’s foot Fungal infection between toes.
Aspergillosis Rare infection caused by mold inhaled into lungs.
Candidiasis Yeast infection caused by Candida species.
Oral thrush Yeast infection in the mouth, common in infants.
Vaginal candidiasis Yeast infection in the vagina.

Study trap:

Yeast tends to thrive in warm, moist areas — not cold, dry areas.

23. Viral infections

General viral examples

Virus/disease Study notes
Ebola Rare and generally fatal; transmitted by contact with body fluids of infected people; early symptoms can include fever, vomiting, diarrhea; late disease can involve internal bleeding and organ failure.
Influenza Highly contagious viral respiratory infection; seasonal; symptoms include fever, sore throat, aches, cough, runny nose, fatigue; vaccine available yearly.
Measles / rubeola Acute, highly contagious respiratory droplet infection; fever, runny nose, cough, rash; can cause bronchitis and permanent hearing loss; cases rising with unvaccinated children.
Mumps Swelling of parotid glands near ears; in adults may cause painful swelling of ovaries or testicles.
Rubella / German measles Low-grade fever, swollen glands, fine pink rash; serious during early pregnancy because fetal defects can occur.
Rabies Transmitted through bite/saliva of infected animal; testing/post-exposure treatment should start quickly if risk suspected; after symptoms develop, usually fatal.
West Nile virus Mosquito-borne; mild flu-like illness or severe brain/spinal-cord spread.
Zika virus Mosquito-borne or transmitted sexually/from pregnant person to fetus; can cause fever/rash/birth defects.

MMR

The measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccination can prevent all three illnesses and is administered in early childhood.

24. Herpesviruses

The herpesvirus group includes:

  • varicella zoster
  • Epstein-Barr virus
  • cytomegalovirus
  • herpes simplex

Cytomegalovirus (CMV)

  • Found in most body fluids.
  • Commonly silent in adults.
  • Can cause serious illness when immune system is weakened.
  • Can transmit from mother to unborn child and cause congenital disability.

Word parts:

  • cyt/o = cell
  • megal/o = large
  • vir = virus
  • -us = singular noun ending

Varicella / chickenpox

  • Caused by varicella zoster virus.
  • Highly contagious.
  • Fever plus hundreds of itchy, fluid-filled blisters.
  • Virus can stay dormant and later reactivate.

Herpes zoster / shingles

  • Reactivation of dormant varicella zoster virus.
  • Painful skin eruptions follow route of inflamed nerve.
  • Vaccine recommended for older adults.

Postherpetic neuralgia

  • Complication after herpes zoster if nerve fibers are damaged.
  • Pain can be severe and persistent.

Infectious mononucleosis / mono

  • Caused by Epstein-Barr virus.
  • Common in adolescents and young adults.
  • Characterized by fever, sore throat, enlarged lymph nodes.
  • Spleen or liver swelling can develop.

Roseola / sixth disease

  • Common in infants 6 months to 2 years.
  • High fever followed by rash.

25. Medications to control infections

Medication type Meaning Examples/notes
Antibiotic Inhibits growth or kills pathogenic bacteria. Effective against most bacterial infections, not viral infections.
Bactericide Causes death of bacteria. Penicillins and cephalosporins.
Bacteriostatic agent Slows/stops bacterial growth. Sulfonamide, erythromycin.
Antifungal Destroys or inhibits fungal growth. Lotrimin for athlete’s foot; also called antimycotic.
Antiviral drug Treats viral infections or provides temporary immunity in some contexts. anti- + vir + -al.

Test trap:

Antibiotics do not treat viral infections like influenza or herpes zoster.

26. Oncology overview

Malignant tumor

A malignant tumor is a form of cancer that can spread to distant body sites and can become life-threatening.

Myosarcoma

  • Malignant tumor from muscle tissue.
  • Word parts: my/o muscle + sarc flesh + -oma tumor.

Cancer

Cancer is a class of diseases characterized by uncontrolled cell division and the ability of cells to invade tissues by:

  1. Direct growth into adjacent tissue.
  2. Spread to distant sites by metastasis.

Metastasize vs metastasis

Term Meaning
metastasize Verb: to spread from one site to another.
metastasis Process or new tumor site; plural = metastases.

Common metastatic sites mentioned:

  • bones
  • liver
  • lungs

27. Tumor growth, blood supply, and staging

Angiogenesis

Angiogenesis = process by which a tumor supports growth by creating its own blood supply.

Word parts:

  • angi/o = vessel
  • -genesis = reproduction/formation

Antiangiogenesis

Antiangiogenesis = treatment approach that disrupts tumor blood supply.

Staging tumors

Staging classifies tumors by:

  • how far disease has progressed
  • potential response to therapy
  • patient prognosis

Staging may use letters, numbers, or Roman numerals.

Example from colorectal-cancer figure:

Stage Simple meaning
Stage I Tumor in polyp/submucosa, has not spread far into tissue.
Stage II Cancer has invaded underlying tissue.
Stage III Cancer spread to nearby lymph nodes.

29. Cancer treatments

The chapter lists the three common broad treatment forms:

  1. Surgery
  2. Chemotherapy
  3. Radiation therapy

Surgery

  • Usually removes malignancy plus margin of surrounding normal tissue.
  • May remove nearby lymph nodes to check spread.
  • Other surgery types include laser surgery and cryosurgery.
Type Main idea
Laser surgery Uses targeted light beams to destroy cancer cells.
Cryosurgery Freezes/destroys cancer cells, often using liquid nitrogen.

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy uses chemical agents/drugs selected to destroy malignant cells and tissues.

Related terms:

Term Meaning
Chemoprevention Natural or synthetic substances/drugs/vitamins used to lower cancer risk, recurrence risk, or slow/size-reduce tumor growth.
Antineoplastic Medication that blocks development, growth, or proliferation of malignant cells.
Proliferation Rapid increase.
Cytotoxic drugs Kill/damage cells; used in immunosuppression and chemotherapy.

Radiation therapy

Goal: destroy cancerous tissue while sparing healthy tissue.

Type Meaning
Brachytherapy Radioactive material placed in contact with or implanted into tissue being treated. brachy- = short.
Teletherapy Radiation delivered from a distance outside the body; 3D imaging can improve targeting.
Tomotherapy Combines tomography with radiation therapy; radiation delivered slice by slice to precisely target tumor and avoid healthy tissue.

Quick answer key

  1. Absorb fats/fat-soluble vitamins; remove waste/cooperate with immunity; return filtered lymph to venous blood.
  2. Fats and fat-soluble vitamins in small-intestine villi.
  3. Interstitial fluid is between cells; lymph is that fluid once inside lymphatic vessels.
  4. Lymphatic system has no pump-like organ.
  5. Lymph, pathogens, foreign material, debris, and abnormal cells.
  6. Natural killer cells, B cells, T cells.
  7. Antibodies; activated B cells can become plasma cells.
  8. Thymus.
  9. Activate immune defenses, slow viral multiplication, signal cells.
  10. Divide/proliferate.
  11. Adenoids/nasopharyngeal, palatine, lingual.
  12. Above heart; largest around puberty then shrinks with age.
  13. Spleen injury can bleed and is a medical emergency.
  14. Enlarged spleen.
  15. Detect damage/malformation of lymphatic vessels.
  16. Primary is hereditary; secondary follows vessel damage from cancer treatment/surgery/trauma/burns/injury.
  17. Control swelling and support circulation/lymph flow.
  18. Severe systemic allergic reaction/anaphylactic shock.
  19. Specific allergens.
  20. Immune attack against the body’s own tissues.
  21. Examples: rheumatoid arthritis—joints/connective tissue; myasthenia gravis—nerve/muscle synapses; multiple sclerosis—brain/spinal cord; Graves’—thyroid; Crohn’s—intestine.
  22. T cells.
  23. Advanced/fatal stage of HIV infection.
  24. Infection by a pathogen that usually does not sicken healthy hosts but does in weakened hosts.
  25. HIV antibodies and other infectious conditions such as Lyme disease.
  26. Cancer: stimulate immune response; allergy: reduce sensitivity/desensitize.
  27. Lab-produced antibodies from a clone of specific cells.
  28. Antibiotics target bacteria, not viruses.
  29. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, resistant to many antibiotics.
  30. Chickenpox/varicella; later shingles/herpes zoster.
  31. Persistent severe nerve pain after shingles.
  32. Measles, mumps, rubella.
  33. Tumor creates blood supply to support growth.
  34. How far tumor/disease has progressed and prognosis/therapy planning.
  35. Brachytherapy places radiation material close/inside tissue; teletherapy delivers radiation from outside body.

34. 45-minute study plan

First 10 minutes: map the system

Draw:

interstitial fluid → lymphatic capillaries → lymph vessels → lymph nodes → ducts → venous blood

Then label: lacteals, tonsils, thymus, spleen, bone marrow, lymph nodes.

Next 10 minutes: immune cells and chemicals

Recite:

  • B cells → antibodies/plasma cells
  • T cells → thymus/cell-mediated immunity/cytokines
  • NK cells → virus-infected and cancer cells
  • macrophages → eat invaders/dead cells
  • dendritic cells → alert B/T cells
  • complement → helps antibodies destroy pathogens

Next 10 minutes: disorders

Review:

  • lymphedema
  • autoimmune disease
  • SCID
  • HIV/AIDS
  • opportunistic infections
  • anaphylaxis
  • MRSA

Next 10 minutes: infections and treatments

Sort:

  • bacteria → antibiotics/bactericides/bacteriostatic meds
  • fungi → antifungals/antimycotics
  • viruses → antivirals, vaccines, immune therapies
  • parasites → malaria/toxoplasmosis examples

Last 5 minutes: oncology

Say out loud:

  • benign vs malignant
  • metastasize vs metastasis
  • angiogenesis vs antiangiogenesis
  • staging
  • surgery, chemotherapy, radiation therapy
  • brachytherapy, teletherapy, tomotherapy
