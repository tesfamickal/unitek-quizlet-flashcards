# Dosage and Equivalents Study Guide — Calculating Conversions

Source video: <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xJvDNtk6pNk>

Video focus: a nursing dosage-calculation webinar on **calculating conversions** for nursing school. The instructor emphasizes safe, fast conversion thinking: know the common equivalents, decide whether the target unit is bigger or smaller, then multiply or divide by the correct conversion factor.

This page is an original study guide based on the video transcript plus our existing dosage-equivalents note. It is designed for quick nursing-math practice and medication-safety review.

---

## 1. Core safety mindset

Dosage calculation is not “math for math’s sake.” In nursing, the goal is:

> **Client safety: prevent medication and fluid-balance errors by converting units correctly.**

When a question asks for a medication dose, intake/output total, infusion time, or military-time end point, the safest habit is:

1. Identify the starting unit.
2. Identify the requested ending unit.
3. Decide whether you are moving from a **bigger unit to a smaller unit** or from a **smaller unit to a bigger unit**.
4. Use the correct conversion factor.
5. Check whether the answer makes real-world sense.

---

## 2. The master rule: bigger to smaller vs smaller to bigger

The video’s main shortcut is simple:

| Direction | What happens | Operation | Memory picture |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bigger unit → smaller unit | More pieces | Multiply | One pizza cut into many slices. |
| Smaller unit → bigger unit | Fewer pieces | Divide | Many slices combined into one pizza. |

Examples:

- kg → g: bigger to smaller → multiply.
- mg → g: smaller to bigger → divide.
- L → mL: bigger to smaller → multiply.
- mL → L: smaller to bigger → divide.
- hours → minutes: bigger to smaller → multiply by 60.
- minutes → hours: smaller to bigger → divide by 60.

> The operation rule stays the same, but the “magic number” changes depending on the conversion. Sometimes it is 1000, sometimes 60, 30, 15, 5, 2.2, 8, etc.

## 3. Metric system: what to actually memorize for nursing

The full King Henry ladder can be useful, but for nursing dosage calculations the video focuses on the most common units.

### High-yield metric equivalents

| Equivalent | Use it for |
|---|---|
| 1 kg = 1000 g | body weight or metric mass |
| 1 g = 1000 mg | medication mass |
| 1 mg = 1000 mcg | small medication doses |
| 1 L = 1000 mL | fluids, IVs, intake/output |

### Metric ladder for common nursing mass units

```text
kg → g → mg → mcg
```

Each step to the right multiplies by 1000.
Each step to the left divides by 1000.

### Metric volume ladder

```text
L → mL
```

L to mL: multiply by 1000.
mL to L: divide by 1000.

## 4. King Henry memory trick with micrograms

The video references the classic metric memory aid:

```text
King Henry died by drinking chocolate milk
```

For nursing, the useful “twist” is remembering that **milli to micro** is three places to the right:

```text
milli → micro = move 3 places right = multiply by 1000
```

Examples:

- 1 mg = 1000 mcg
- 0.5 mg = 500 mcg
- 2 mg = 2000 mcg

Avoid this trap:

> **mg and mcg are not the same.** A 1000-fold error can be dangerous.

## 5. Metric examples from the video

### Example 1: 3.4 kg to grams

kg is bigger than g, so multiply by 1000.

```text
3.4 kg × 1000 = 3400 g
```

Answer: **3400 g**

### Example 2: 1500 mg to grams

mg is smaller than g, so divide by 1000.

```text
1500 mg ÷ 1000 = 1.5 g
```

Answer: **1.5 g**

### Example 3: 500 mcg to milligrams

mcg is smaller than mg, so divide by 1000.

```text
500 mcg ÷ 1000 = 0.5 mg
```

Answer: **0.5 mg**

### Example 4: 2.5 L to milliliters

L is bigger than mL, so multiply by 1000.

```text
2.5 L × 1000 = 2500 mL
```

Answer: **2500 mL**

### Example 5: 750 mL to liters

mL is smaller than L, so divide by 1000.

```text
750 mL ÷ 1000 = 0.75 L
```

Answer: **0.75 L**

## 6. Household-to-metric liquid conversions

The video teaches the common nursing approximations, not exact chemistry-lab conversions.

### “1 T table ounce” memory trick

Say:

```text
1 T table ounce — 5, 15, 30
```

Meaning:

| Household unit | Metric equivalent |
|---|---:|
| 1 teaspoon / tsp / t | 5 mL |
| 1 tablespoon / tbsp / T | 15 mL |
| 1 fluid ounce / fl oz | 30 mL |

Important notes:

- These are standard nursing-school approximations.
- A teaspoon is technically about 4.93 mL, but nursing dosage problems usually use **5 mL**.
- Capital **T** often means tablespoon; lowercase **t** often means teaspoon. Read carefully.

## 7. Household volume hierarchy: Queen Gallon

The video’s memory phrase:

```text
Good Queens Prefer Chocolate Fondue
```

This gives the order from larger to smaller:

```text
Gallon → Quart → Pint → Cup → Fluid ounce
```

### Queen Gallon chart

| Equivalent | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 gallon = 4 quarts | Queen Gallon has four “limbs.” |
| 1 quart = 2 pints | Each quart splits into two pints. |
| 1 pint = 2 cups | Each pint splits into two cups. |
| 1 cup = 8 fluid ounces | Think octopus: 8 fluid-ounce “tentacles.” |
| 1 fluid ounce = 30 mL | Bridge from household volume to metric. |

### Derived equivalents

| Unit | mL equivalent |
|---|---:|
| 1 tsp | 5 mL |
| 1 tbsp | 15 mL |
| 1 fl oz | 30 mL |
| 1 cup | 240 mL |
| 1 pint | 480 mL |
| 1 quart | 960 mL |
| 1 gallon | 3840 mL |

Note: some courses round differently for larger volumes, but this video uses the standard nursing shortcut of **1 fl oz = 30 mL** and therefore **1 cup = 240 mL**.

## 8. Weight conversion: kg and lb

Common nursing conversion:

```text
1 kg = 2.2 lb
```

Use this for weight-based medication calculations.

| Direction | Operation |
|---|---|
| kg → lb | multiply by 2.2 |
| lb → kg | divide by 2.2 |

Examples:

```text
70 kg × 2.2 = 154 lb
154 lb ÷ 2.2 = 70 kg
```

Safety note:

> Many weight-based medication orders require kg. If the chart gives lb, convert to kg before calculating dose.

## 9. Intake and output: why conversions matter

The video connects household conversions to intake/output documentation.

In nursing questions, intake and output are often documented in **mL**.

### Intake examples

Intake may include:

- water
- juice
- coffee/tea
- soup
- IV fluids
- liquid medications
- tube feeding

### Output examples

Output may include:

- urine
- emesis/vomiting
- diarrhea or measured stool output
- wound drainage
- suction drainage

### Net balance formula

```text
net balance = total intake − total output
```

| Result | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Positive net balance | More fluid in than out; possible fluid overload depending on context. |
| Negative net balance | More fluid out than in; possible fluid deficit/dehydration depending on context. |

## 10. Intake/output example from the video

The video walks through a 12-hour shift example.

### Intake items

| Item | Conversion | mL |
|---|---|---:|
| 2 cups water | 2 cups × 8 fl oz/cup × 30 mL/fl oz | 480 mL |
| Continuous IV at 50 mL/hr for 12 hr | 50 × 12 | 600 mL |
| 12 fl oz fluid | 12 × 30 | 360 mL |
| 3 tsp liquid med | 3 × 5 | 15 mL |
| **Total intake** | 480 + 600 + 360 + 15 | **1455 mL** |

### Output items

| Item | Conversion | mL |
|---|---|---:|
| Urine | already mL | 850 mL |
| Two bowel movements totaling 500 mL | “totaling” means combined amount, not each | 500 mL |
| 6 fl oz emesis/drainage | 6 × 30 | 180 mL |
| 0.4 L output | 0.4 × 1000 | 400 mL |
| **Total output** | 850 + 500 + 180 + 400 | **1930 mL** |

### Net balance

```text
1455 mL intake − 1930 mL output = -475 mL
```

Answer: **-475 mL net balance**

Interpretation: the client had more output than intake, which suggests a fluid deficit trend. Whether it is clinically dehydration depends on the whole patient situation.

### Wording trap

> “Two bowel movements totaling 500 mL” means the two together equal 500 mL. It does **not** mean 500 mL each.

## 11. Time conversions

Dosage calculations often use time for infusion duration, mL/hr, start/end times, and charting.

### Basic time equivalents

| Equivalent | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 minute = 60 seconds | min ↔ sec |
| 1 hour = 60 minutes | hr ↔ min |
| 1 day = 24 hours | not 12 hours |

Same operation rule:

| Direction | Operation |
|---|---|
| hours → minutes | multiply by 60 |
| minutes → hours | divide by 60 |
| minutes → seconds | multiply by 60 |
| seconds → minutes | divide by 60 |

## 12. AM/PM to military time

### AM times

For AM times, the military time usually matches the hour with a leading zero if needed.

| Standard time | Military time |
|---|---|
| 12:00 a.m. | 0000 |
| 7:00 a.m. | 0700 |
| 9:00 a.m. | 0900 |
| 11:00 a.m. | 1100 |

### PM times

For PM times after 12:00 p.m., add 12 to the hour.

| Standard time | Work | Military time |
|---|---|---|
| 12:00 p.m. | noon stays 12 | 1200 |
| 4:00 p.m. | 4 + 12 = 16 | 1600 |
| 9:00 p.m. | 9 + 12 = 21 | 2100 |
| 9:45 p.m. | 9 + 12 = 21 | 2145 |

Rule:

```text
PM after noon → add 12 to the hour
```

Do not add 12 to 12:00 p.m.

---

## 13. Military time to AM/PM

If the military hour is greater than 12, subtract 12 and label it p.m.

| Military time | Work | Standard time |
|---|---|---|
| 0720 | hour is not over 12 | 7:20 a.m. |
| 1300 | 13 − 12 = 1 | 1:00 p.m. |
| 1900 | 19 − 12 = 7 | 7:00 p.m. |
| 2145 | 21 − 12 = 9 | 9:45 p.m. |

Rules:

- 0000 = midnight / start of new day.
- 1200 = noon.
- 1300–2359 = subtract 12 from the hour and label p.m.

## 14. Decimal hours to hours and minutes

A decimal after hours is not automatically minutes.

### Key rule

```text
decimal part of hours × 60 = minutes
```

### Example: 6.25 hours

Do not write 6 hr 25 min.

```text
0.25 × 60 = 15 min
```

Answer: **6 hr 15 min**

Memory hook:

> 0.25 hour is a quarter of an hour. “Quarter after” means 15 minutes.

### Common decimal-hour conversions

| Decimal hours | Minutes |
|---:|---:|
| 0.25 hr | 15 min |
| 0.5 hr | 30 min |
| 0.75 hr | 45 min |
| 0.1 hr | 6 min |
| 0.2 hr | 12 min |
| 0.9 hr | 54 min |

---

## 15. Hours and minutes to decimal hours

If you have hours and minutes and need decimal hours:

```text
minutes ÷ 60 = decimal portion of hour
```

### Example: 10 hr 54 min to hours

Do not write 10.54 hr.

```text
54 ÷ 60 = 0.9
10 hr + 0.9 hr = 10.9 hr
```

Answer: **10.9 hr**

Reality check:

> 54 minutes is close to a full hour, so the decimal should be close to 1.0, not 0.54.

## 16. Infusion finish-time example

Video example:

```text
Infusion starts at 2140.
It is expected to finish in 6.75 hours.
What time will it finish?
```

### Step 1: Convert 6.75 hr to hours and minutes

```text
0.75 × 60 = 45 min
6.75 hr = 6 hr 45 min
```

### Step 2: Convert 2140 to standard time if helpful

```text
21 − 12 = 9
2140 = 9:40 p.m.
```

### Step 3: Add time

```text
9:40 p.m. + 6 hr = 3:40 a.m.
3:40 a.m. + 45 min = 4:25 a.m.
```

Answer:

```text
0425
```

Why it is not 0325:

- 40 min + 45 min = 85 min.
- 85 min = 1 hr + 25 min.
- Carry the extra hour into the hour column.

## 18. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap | Safe correction |
|---|---|
| Treating 6.25 hr as 6 hr 25 min | Multiply .25 × 60 = 15 min. |
| Treating 10 hr 54 min as 10.54 hr | Divide 54 ÷ 60 = 0.9, so 10.9 hr. |
| Forgetting to carry extra minutes over to hours | If minutes ≥ 60, subtract 60 and add 1 hour. |
| Confusing tsp and tbsp | tsp = 5 mL; tbsp = 15 mL. |
| Confusing oz and fl oz | oz = weight; fl oz = liquid volume. |
| Multiplying when moving smaller → bigger | Smaller to bigger means divide. |
| Dividing when moving bigger → smaller | Bigger to smaller means multiply. |
| Reading “totaling” as “each” | Totaling means combined amount. |
| Forgetting noon/midnight exceptions | 0000 = midnight; 1200 = noon. |
